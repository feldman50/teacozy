AI to Reduce Workflow Friction

High-volume, repetitive classification and routing tasks were creating a sustained bottleneck in a customer-facing workflow. The instinct was to automate — but automation without trust would create a different kind of problem. I led the initiative to introduce AI assistance into this workflow in a way that reduced friction without trading reliability for speed. The approach was disciplined: identify where AI confidence was high, make failure visible and recoverable, and earn user trust incrementally before increasing automation scope.

The Problem

Customer-submitted requests required manual classification before they could be routed to the right team. Volume was high, variance was broad, and the classification criteria had accumulated years of informal edge cases. Experienced staff handled it well; newer staff needed significant training time and still made classification errors that caused delays downstream.

The bottleneck was expensive in two ways: it slowed response time for customers and it required a disproportionate share of experienced staff time on a task that didn't require judgment in most cases. The 20% of requests that needed real judgment were consuming the same processing overhead as the 80% that were straightforward.

The Strategy

The strategy was constrained from the start: AI assistance only, not AI replacement. The goal was to let AI handle high-confidence, routine cases and surface low-confidence or ambiguous cases for human review — clearly, with enough context for the reviewer to make a fast decision.

This framing mattered for user acceptance. Staff were skeptical — reasonably so, given that errors in classification had real consequences for customers. Rather than asserting that the AI would be accurate, we made accuracy measurable and visible from the start. Users could see confidence scores, disagree with suggestions, and flag patterns the model was getting consistently wrong. Their corrections fed the improvement cycle.

The Execution

The first phase was observation only — the model ran in shadow mode alongside the existing manual process for eight weeks. We measured agreement rate between AI suggestions and human decisions, identified the request types where AI confidence was consistently high, and found the categories where the model needed more signal.

Phase two introduced AI suggestions as visible recommendations in the review interface, with confidence indicators and a one-click override. Users saw the suggestion and made the final call. This built familiarity and generated correction data simultaneously.

Phase three enabled auto-routing for the subset of request types where model confidence exceeded a validated threshold and historical override rates were low. Auto-routed requests remained auditable and reversible. The threshold for auto-routing was set conservatively and reviewed quarterly with the operations team.

The Outcome

Manual review time per request dropped meaningfully for the high-confidence categories, freeing experienced staff to focus on the complex cases that actually required judgment. Customer response time improved in the categories where auto-routing was enabled. Error rates remained low — importantly, lower than the manual-only baseline for the auto-routed subset — because the model was consistently handling the cases it was well-suited for. Staff reported higher satisfaction with the tool than expected, attributing it to the transparency of the confidence scoring and the retained ability to override.
