Cost Continuity Across Enterprise Workflows

Enterprise teams were making financial decisions from contradictory numbers. Procurement, finance, and project management each maintained separate cost records with no reliable way to reconcile them. I led the cross-functional work to define and ship a shared cost-data layer that propagated a single cost record across five connected systems — eliminating the reconciliation burden and creating a foundation for more accurate financial forecasting.

The Problem

Cost data was entered at the point of purchase, again at project kickoff, again during invoicing, and again during close-out reporting. Each team owned a different slice of the process and a different system of record. When costs changed — and they always did — the change rarely propagated cleanly downstream. Finance would close the books on numbers that procurement hadn't yet updated. Project managers were carrying manual spreadsheets to bridge the gap.

The downstream effects were significant: forecasts drifted from actuals, leadership decisions were made on stale data, and finance spent a disproportionate amount of time on reconciliation rather than analysis. No single team owned the problem — each owned one part of it.

The Strategy

Rather than consolidating all systems into one (politically and technically infeasible), the strategy was to define a canonical cost record that each system would read from and write to, rather than maintaining its own. This required agreeing on a shared data model across five teams, each with existing contracts and ingrained workflows.

Getting there required reframing the conversation: this wasn't a technology project, it was an alignment project. Each team needed to see that a shared source of truth benefited them more than owning their own copy. I spent the first two months facilitating that conversation before writing a single product spec.

The Execution

Delivery was phased to reduce risk. Phase one connected procurement and finance — the two systems with the most frequent reconciliation conflicts. We shipped a read-only integration first so finance teams could see procurement data without changing any existing workflows. This built confidence and surfaced edge cases before we rewired any write paths.

Phase two extended the model to project management and invoicing. By this point, the data model was proven and the change-management approach was established. I maintained a working group with leads from each affected team that met bi-weekly throughout delivery, which kept issues from becoming blockers and kept leadership informed without requiring escalation.

The Outcome

Finance teams reported a significant reduction in reconciliation time at period close. Forecast accuracy improved because cost changes in procurement now propagated automatically to downstream systems rather than waiting for manual sync. Project managers retired the bridging spreadsheets they had relied on for years. The shared cost layer became a platform primitive that three subsequent initiatives were built on top of — extending its value well beyond the original scope.
